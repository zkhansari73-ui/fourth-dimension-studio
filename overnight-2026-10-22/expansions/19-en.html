Going deeper: designing a narrative exhibition — from curatorial sentence to visitor journey

The work discussed above is one answer to a general problem: how does a space tell a story? Behind any successful narrative exhibition sits a repeatable method that can be learned, briefed and reviewed like any other design discipline. The sequence matters more than any single device: begin with a sentence, turn it into a journey, give the journey rhythm, layer the content for different depths of attention, prototype before building, and design operations as carefully as opening day.

Step one: write the curatorial sentence

Before any sketch, curator and designer agree on one sentence: after leaving, what exactly will the visitor understand differently? The sentence is not a slogan, nor a summary of the content list; it is the contract every later decision must answer to. Any striking image or device that cannot be traced back to it is decoration, however beautiful. Teams that skip this step learn the cost late, when the exhibition impresses but visitors cannot say what it was about.

Step two: storyboard the journey

With the sentence fixed, design the visit as a storyboard, not a floor plan. A journey has three movements: arrival and orientation, immersion in the narrative, and a closing moment to assemble what they have seen. Mark where people stop, where they accelerate, where queues form and where a genuine choice of route exists. An enforced single path feels like a corridor; a clear main street with quieter side lanes invites exploration while delivering the core story to everyone.

Rhythm: pacing the movement

Stories in space are experienced in time, so pacing is a design material. Alternate intensity with rest: a dense, information-rich room lands harder when the next space is quieter, and a spectacular moment needs an approach that builds expectation and an exit that allows reflection. Think in beats, as editors think in scenes: where does the visitor breathe, and where do they lean in? Crowding is a rhythm problem too — if everyone stops at the same point, the beat collapses.

Two speeds of looking: the fast view and the deep view

Visitors bring very different budgets of time and attention, so layer the content. The fast view must work within minutes and from a distance: the overall argument, the landmarks, where to go next. The deep view rewards those who stop and approach: readable texts, fine detail, secondary stories. The test is strict. A fast-view visitor should still leave with the curatorial sentence intact; a visitor who invests an hour should find that the promised depth was real, not a lure.

Prototype the question before the mechanism

When a display depends on movement, interaction or timing, resist engineering the mechanism first. Prototype the question: what should the visitor feel, notice or do at this moment? A full-scale cardboard mock-up, tested with a few real visitors, answers faster and cheaper than any rendering. Only when the moment works in rough form does the mechanism earn serious engineering — with its own accountable brief covering loads, duty cycles, noise, failure behaviour, spare parts and named responsibility when it stops on a busy afternoon.

Operations is part of the design

An exhibition is finished not at opening, but when its full cycle of use is budgeted and staffed. Consumables run out, lamps age, interactive surfaces wear and content needs refreshing; each task needs a named owner. Invite the operations team into the design review as authors, not audience — they know which detail will be cleaned, repaired or quietly removed within a month. Access panels, replaceable parts and documented settings are how a concept survives contact with reality.

Questions for the design review

End with a critique session whose output is a decision list, not a mood. Its questions include:

  • Can a first-time visitor state our curatorial sentence in their own words after one visit?
  • Where does the journey depend on a single path, a single device or a single member of staff?
  • What does the fast view contain, and what is only available to the deep view?
  • Which moment was prototyped with real visitors, and what did the test change?
  • What breaks first, who fixes it, and how long does the exhibition stay honest while broken?
  • Which elements earn their cost across the whole run, not just the opening week?

A narrative exhibition designed this way is not guaranteed to move anyone; no method can promise that. But when it fails, it fails for honest reasons the team can see, discuss and repair — not because nobody ever agreed what the story was.
