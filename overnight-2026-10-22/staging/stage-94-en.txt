<h2>Going deeper: a 90-day AI maturity roadmap for the studio, after the first pilot</h2>
<p>The pilot answered a narrow question: can this workflow work here, on our kind of projects, with our kind of information? The ninety days that follow answer a different and harder one: can the studio absorb the workflow into normal practice without quietly eroding quality, confidentiality, or judgement? Maturity is not about adding more tools. It is about making one proven practice boring, dependable, and teachable — and only then widening it. The roadmap below assumes the pilot ended with a modest success, a documented information boundary, and human review built in as a designed step rather than an afterthought.</p>
<h3>Days 1–30: consolidate what the pilot proved</h3>
<p>The first month is deliberately unglamorous. Take the single pilot workflow and run it under ordinary conditions: real deadlines, real interruptions, the people who were not in the pilot room. Write down what actually happened — where the output needed heavy correction, where review caught something important, where the process saved effort and where it merely moved effort elsewhere. Convert the pilot team's informal habits into a short written procedure: what goes in, what never goes in, who checks, what "good enough to leave the building" looks like. If the workflow cannot survive being written down in one or two pages, it is not ready to spread. This phase also has a quiet purpose: it separates genuine value from first-enthusiasm. A workflow that still looks worthwhile on a tired Tuesday in week four is probably real.</p>
<h3>Days 31–60: standardise the practice</h3>
<p>In the second month the studio turns one team's procedure into a shared standard. That means agreed prompt patterns or templates where they genuinely help, a common place where the approved materials live, and a short onboarding path so a colleague joining mid-project reaches the same standard without shadowing someone for a week. Standardisation is also where the studio decides the exceptions: project types where the workflow is mandatory to offer, types where it is optional, and types where it is off the table entirely — because of client restrictions, confidentiality, or the nature of the work. Naming the exceptions in advance prevents awkward improvisation later, when a deadline is pressing and the tempting shortcut is closest.</p>
<h3>Days 61–90: scale — carefully, and only where it earned it</h3>
<p>Scaling does not mean every project and every task. It means extending the standard to adjacent workflows that resemble the pilot: a second document type, a second project stage, a second team with a named local owner. Each extension is a mini-pilot, with its own boundary check and its own review habit, because a method that behaved well on early-stage material may behave very differently on contractual or client-facing material. By day ninety the studio should be able to say, precisely, where AI assistance is normal practice, where it is experimental, and where it is not used — and be able to explain each boundary to a client in one honest sentence.</p>
<h3>Roles and ownership</h3>
<p>Tools without owners drift. The studio needs one named person — not a committee — accountable for the AI standard: maintaining the procedure, approving changes, and being the obvious first call when something looks wrong. Project leads own the boundary on their projects: they decide what project information may enter which tool, and they own the review step. Everyone who uses the workflow owns their own checks; "the system produced it" is never a transfer of responsibility. These are small allocations of authority, but without them a studio ends up with enthusiastic pockets of practice that nobody can describe, defend, or switch off.</p>
<h3>Governance and client-data rules</h3>
<p>By day ninety the rules should fit on an index card, and every member of the studio should be able to recite them: client material enters only the tools the studio has approved for that sensitivity level; nothing confidential is pasted into a tool whose terms the studio has not read; outputs that will leave the studio are reviewed by a named human; and anything that feels borderline is escalated, not experimented with. Governance is not suspicion of the team — it is what allows the team to work quickly without each person renegotiating the boundaries alone, at midnight, under pressure.</p>
<h3>Evaluating without fake metrics</h3>
<p>Studios are often tempted to justify the programme with invented numbers. Resist. The honest evidence at this stage is qualitative and specific: reviewers' notes on what the review step caught; before-and-after comparisons of the same recurring task, judged by the person responsible for it; a log of incidents and near misses; and the frank answer to one question — knowing what we now know, would we choose this workflow again? A short monthly review of that material tells the studio more than a dashboard of percentages nobody measured. Maturity shows up as better questions, not louder numbers.</p>
<h3>Failure modes when scaling too fast</h3>
<p>The common pattern is not a dramatic failure but a slow leak: the workflow spreads to tasks it was never tested on; the review step quietly becomes a signature rather than a check; project information starts crossing boundaries because "it worked last time"; and new joiners learn the tool before they learn the standard it sits inside. Each leak is small and reasonable in isolation. Together they reconstruct exactly the uncontrolled usage the pilot was designed to replace. The defence is the calendar itself — hold the phases, even when enthusiasm argues otherwise.</p>
<h3>When to say no to a tool</h3>
<p>A mature studio declines tools, and says why. Say no when the terms around client data are unclear or change casually; when the tool demands a broader connection to studio systems than the task requires; when nobody can describe how its output would be reviewed; or when the honest reason for adopting it is that a competitor mentioned it. Every "no" recorded with its reason strengthens the "yes" decisions around it — and that, more than the number of tools in use, is what ninety days of maturity should produce.</p>
